What is Sepsis?

Sepsis is the body's life-threatening, dysregulated response to infection. Rather than the infection itself causing all the harm, it is the immune system's overwhelming reaction that damages the patient's own tissues and organs, potentially leading to septic shock, multi-organ failure, and death if not recognized and treated quickly.

Sepsis can develop from almost any infection — pneumonia, urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, and abdominal infections are among the most common triggers. It can affect anyone, but the very young, the elderly, people with chronic illness, and those who are immunocompromised are at especially high risk.

Sepsis is a medical emergency. Every hour that treatment (particularly antibiotics and fluid resuscitation) is delayed is associated with a measurable increase in mortality. This is why early recognition by frontline staff, not just physicians, is so critical.

Why Early Recognition Is Hard

Sepsis often masquerades as a routine deterioration: a slightly elevated heart rate, a bit more confusion than usual, a low-grade fever. Individually these signs can seem minor, but taken together and tracked against a patient's baseline, they can indicate the earliest stages of sepsis, well before a patient looks obviously critical. This is why standardized screening tools exist — they help catch the pattern even when no single sign stands out on its own.
