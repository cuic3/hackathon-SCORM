Why Hand Hygiene Matters

Hand hygiene is the single most effective action healthcare workers can take to prevent the spread of infection. Hands are the most common vehicle for transmitting pathogens between patients, staff, and equipment in any clinical setting, from a busy emergency department to a quiet long-term care unit.

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) affect millions of patients every year worldwide, extending hospital stays, increasing costs, and in some cases contributing to serious complications or death. Studies consistently show that consistent, correctly performed hand hygiene can reduce transmission of HAIs by a substantial margin, making it one of the highest return-on-effort interventions in patient safety.

Organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) both identify hand hygiene as a foundational patient safety practice, alongside standard precautions such as personal protective equipment and safe injection practices.

Two Accepted Methods

There are two primary ways to perform hand hygiene in a clinical setting:

  • Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR): The preferred method in most situations when hands are not visibly soiled. It is faster, less irritating to skin with repeated use, and more effective against most pathogens than plain soap and water.
  • Soap and water: Required when hands are visibly dirty or soiled with blood or body fluids, and preferred after caring for patients with known or suspected Clostridioides difficile or norovirus, since alcohol alone does not reliably kill these spore-forming or non-enveloped organisms.
